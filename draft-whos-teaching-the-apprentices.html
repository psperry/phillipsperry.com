Who's Teaching the Apprentices?

Experience, judgment, and the people who help us learn.

When I was 19, I went to work as an apprentice electrician for my older brother, Tim.

Tim was a master electrician. He wasn't particularly patient, but he stuck with me.

At first, I wasn't much help. I spent most of my time following him around, watching what he did and trying to anticipate what he'd need next. If I could hand him the right tool before he asked for it, I figured I was making progress.

I remember coming home from work and falling asleep on the sofa. Not because the work was particularly physical, at least not for me. I wasn't doing enough to get physically exhausted.

I was exhausted from thinking.

Everything was new. I had to concentrate on things Tim probably did without giving them much thought.

But gradually, things began to change.

I learned the tools, the materials, and the work. I started recognizing problems and understanding how to solve them. Before long, I was working with other master electricians, and eventually I was supervising others myself.

I've been thinking about those days because something familiar happened to me recently.

I spent an afternoon using AI to audit two years of payroll and payroll liabilities for two clients.

It was remarkably productive. I was able to investigate things that would have taken considerably longer without AI.

And by the time I finished, I was exhausted.

Sound familiar?

AI was doing much of the heavy lifting, but I was still responsible for the thinking. I had to question its conclusions, recognize inconsistencies, investigate discrepancies, and decide what to do next.

I couldn't simply accept what it told me. Some of its answers were wrong, and some were convincing enough that an inexperienced person might not have recognized the problems.

I expect I'll get better at working this way. I'll learn where to concentrate my attention, what to question, and what I can safely delegate.

Just as I did when I was 19.

But there's an important difference.

When I was learning electrical work, I had Tim.

He knew things I didn't know. He could recognize when I was doing something wrong, sometimes before I realized it myself. And eventually, he trusted me to do more on my own.

Today, AI can help someone with very little experience produce work that looks like it came from an expert.

That's pretty amazing. It's also a little concerning.

I've been wondering how people starting their careers will develop the judgment to recognize when AI gets something wrong. And how their supervisors will find the time to help them when everyone is producing so much more work.

In accounting, we're looking at ways to independently verify the accuracy of the books without requiring managers to repeat everything their bookkeepers have done.

Part of the appeal is obvious. We can catch mistakes and give managers more confidence in the work.

But there's another possibility that interests me more.

If managers spend less time looking for mistakes, maybe they'll have more time to help their people understand them.

That's something Tim did for me, although I'm not sure either of us thought of it as teaching at the time.

Much of what I learned came from watching him work, making mistakes, and gradually figuring out why he did things the way he did.

AI is changing how we work. I think it's going to make many of us considerably more productive.

But I don't think experience and judgment are becoming less important. And I don't think people will develop those qualities simply by having access to better tools.

Someone still has to teach the apprentices.

Tim wasn't particularly patient, and I'm sure I tested what patience he had.

But he stuck with me.

I'm glad he did.
